Preface

Reflection and Choice

I first knew America as a cowboy. Frontier, Kansas, independence, a man on a horse against an empty horizon. No matter how stereotypical, it caught something real, even if I couldn't name it yet.

Last summer, in Stanford's library, I found James Madison's annotations in the margins of Paradise Lost. I was there to read about the early republic history of American Enlightenment. Yet I was lost in its future. Milton was asking what human beings do with freedom. A century later, Hamilton would ask whether a people could establish its government through "reflection and choice," rather than accident and force.

The cowboy was only a myth, beyond those who actually toiled at the Frontier. Behind it was an almost classical thesis: whether human agency could bend the future, or whether we are forever carried by forces we didn't choose.

I have spent much of my life waiting for things I could not control. It didn't teach me to predict the future. It taught me to prepare for it, and, when the moment came, to choose. This portfolio is a record of those choices: the things I observed, the people I joined, and the new press I am still looking for.

Part I

Press

Tesla in China: An Interactive Map

Live demo

The paper's thesis is invisible in a table. Supply-chain integration, not policy, determines Tesla's provincial market share. I built a choropleth map with a toggle: BEV or NEV denominator. Under BEV, Xinjiang looks like Tesla's strongest market. Switch to NEV, it collapses. The Yangtze Delta snaps into focus. The discrepancy is a PHEV composition artifact. I found the bug in our own analysis, then built the button that shows it.

First map I ever coded. Learned Leaflet.js from documentation. The GeoJSON boundaries needed manual matching to 30 province codes, one by one.

A toggle is an argument. The BEV/NEV switch doesn't just display two datasets. It forces the viewer to ask why they differ. That question is the paper's thesis in one click.

One Step Forward, Two Steps Back

Georgetown Labor History Resource Project · Live on Georgetown's site · Source

I cold-emailed Patrick Dixon at Georgetown with a pitch deck. No institutional affiliation, no faculty sponsor. The bet was that the gap in their archive, no Asian migrant labor section, was large enough that they'd let a high school student fill it. They did. I spent months in the Library of Congress recovering thirty forgotten strikes and unionization drives from contemporary newspapers and union yearbooks, then built an interactive StoryMap tracing the Pacific labor corridor from Hawaiʻi through California to the Pacific Northwest. It is now live on Georgetown's site.

Meetings with the director were almost weekly. Meetings with a Stanford designer whose Pacific railroad StoryMap became my template happened at whatever hour we could find. Once at five in the morning Beijing time, because she had just finished work in New York.

Primary sources don't argue. They accumulate. Thirty incidents, individually minor, collectively undeniable. The StoryMap's job is to make that accumulation visible in space.

Jesuit Corpus Explorer

Live prototype · Part of Mapping Classical Reception in Beijing

When a Jesuit missionary describes himself in a private Latin petition to his General, and later in a letter edited and published for French readers, does the self he presents change? To work toward an answer I built a pipeline that scores Jesuit writing on seven dimensions of self-description: spiritual interiority, bodily vulnerability, mission desire, obedience, cultural encounter, scientific self, and eschatological time. The explorer shows 18 published letters from the Lettres édifiantes, normalized per 1,000 words so that long and short letters can be compared. What it shows today is how much published letters differ from one another. That spread is the baseline. A gap between private and published writing only means something if it is larger than the gap between two published letters.

The private side of the comparison is not here yet. It needs Indipetae by the same authors, held in the Jesuit archives in Rome. I would rather show an empty slot than a misleading gap.

For a museum-kiosk version I wrote a product spec first. One rule came before any interface work: any passage the model cites must appear word for word in the source, or it is dropped. A fabricated quotation next to real Latin is the one mistake a specialist catches at once.

在场 Present

Podcast · Episode 1 on Spotify · Episode 2 on 小宇宙

Started with a conversation my chemistry teacher said shouldn't just stay in the room. The first episode brought together my English teacher, a writer who has lived across Eastern Europe, China, and America, and a Columbia-trained political economy PhD. We talked about crossing as privilege and crossing as necessity, the Republic of Letters as the world's first internet, why your second language is where you fight better. The second, with Erika Valdivieso from Yale Classics, traces Virgil's reception in Latin America and what public humanities might become when it stops being a Western monologue. A third conversation, with Julian Posada from Yale American Studies on shared labor rights violations in China's and Latin America's data annotation industries, is recorded and not yet released. More labor scholars from LAWCHA to follow.

Recording is the opposite of writing. A paper hides behind its footnotes. A conversation exposes you in real time. Two episodes are out and a third is recorded. No studio, no editor, no institution. Just a microphone and a question worth asking out loud.

Part II

Observations

Between Ritual and Craft: Guild Corporatism in Late Imperial China

75th Midwest Conference on Asian Affairs · 2026 Southwest Conference on Asian Studies · Accepted

This paper asks a question that can be traced back to the Great Divergence: why did the most organized urban communities in late imperial China produce nothing resembling civic autonomy? The guilds had literate leadership, collective welfare, and the capacity to govern urban life. By every comparative metric, especially the Western European ones, they should have generated civil society. They didn't. Not simply because of despotism, not because of commercial stagnation. Because their own ritual hierarchies, patron-deity worship, apprenticeship ceremony, native-place obligation, reproduced imperial authority from the inside. The more institutionally sophisticated they became, the more perfectly they replicated the structure they might have challenged.

The hardest move was arguing against both sides. Weber says Chinese despotism crushed civic life. William Rowe and Rankin say civic life flourished. I'm saying neither. The constraint was internal, and neither camp had recognized it.

Organization does not equal liberation. This sentence has shaped everything I've done since.

Floating Antiquities, Contested Heavens: Jesuit Cosmology and State Authority

2026 Janus Conference · Accepted

The 1665 Calendar Case is usually told as science versus tradition. I read it as a collision between two equally constructed antiquities, one Confucian, one Aristotelian, each claiming a classical past neither actually possessed. Yang Guangxian's indictment borrows its moral authority from Mencius. A sage-level posture adopted by a minor official, recognizably presumptuous to contemporary readers. When Verbiest prevailed, he won not with the system Yang had attacked but with one that had already quietly abandoned its own classical foundations. Neither antiquity survived the contest intact. The winner had departed from the authority invoked in its name.

The case repays attention not as a China-West story but as a study in how institutional actors perform certainty while working around constraints they cannot admit out loud. A dynamic that did not end in 1742.

Land Transfer and Rural Governance in Baoji, Shaanxi

Independent fieldwork

I went home. After reading Mattingly's The Art of Political Control in China, I designed independent fieldwork in my family's home region. North and South Wushao villages in Baoji, the rust-belt city where my uncle's Volkswagen navigates emptying streets. I interviewed village cadres, cooperative managers, and farmers. They knew my family. I had no institutional authority, but they had no reason to perform for me either. What I found was not the static bureaucracy I'd expected. Villagers quoted anti-corruption slogans they'd seen on village walls to win land disputes. The regime's language, turned against its own agents.

Theory looks different from the ground. Scott and O'Brien are precise categories, until you watch a farmer deploy them without having read either.

Inside China's Data Annotation Chain

Fieldwork · Data Workers' Inquiry

The essay tells the WeChat group, the missing contract, the recruiter who said he too was merely a contractor. What it doesn't tell is what I did next. I cold-emailed Dr. Milagros Miceli of the Data Workers' Inquiry and met online. She walked me through how Kenyan annotators had converted informal organizing into unionization, and unionization into the foundation for legislative advocacy. The model was clear: collective bargaining first, legal infrastructure second, governance framework third. In China, step one is structurally blocked. The dispersion that makes gig work feel like freedom is precisely what prevents the organizing that everything else depends on. I left with a map of what's missing, not just a description of what's broken. A recorded but not yet released podcast conversation with Julian Posada extends this question into Latin America.

I identified a vulnerability no governance framework I'd encountered had named: because English annotation disproportionately attracts academically proficient students, the informal WeChat recruitment channel is a de facto pipeline into data labor for minors. No age verification, no filtering, no visibility in international AI frameworks.

Part III

Associations

Frank

Peer mentorship · 2024–present

I was assigned to tutor a sophomore named Frank Zhang in AP English. A student from outside Beijing, navigating migration and the absence of sustained mentorship.

I started a reading group partly to prove something: that through Socratic questioning, students like Frank could be cultivated into political awareness. During our meetings, Frank would listen while others debated current affairs and remain silent. I kept throwing out questions, waiting for the response that would show my method was working.

Our real conversations only began after I stopped asking. Riding the bus home with milk tea, we discovered a shared love of jazz. From Miles Davis and improvisation, the talk wandered toward Frank's childhood in Ürümqi. The lamb chops, the drinking culture, the parts of his family history he never mentioned. Growing up with his grandfather, a senior commander in Xinjiang Bingtuan, he remembered dinner-table references he didn't understand as a child. Only later did he realize what those fragments belonged to.

I had thought his silence was something to overcome. He didn't avoid politics because he had never thought about it. He had thought about it enough to know how painful choosing a side could be.

He signed up for the Chinese Young Physicists' Tournament. I asked how his physics was. "Average." "Then why are you signing up?" He thought for a moment. "Sounds interesting." So I helped. Reading fluid dynamics papers, watching COMSOL guides late into the evening. He earned a place on the varsity team, became its student leader, now mentors younger participants himself. While preparing for CYPT, he taught me physics I would never have explored alone.

Last month we visited the Beijing Ancient Observatory. Standing in front of the armillary sphere, Western astronomy brought by Jesuit missionaries, integrated with Jieqi, the Chinese tradition of celestial observation.

"I wish today's NSF and Chinese Academy of Sciences could do the same," I joked.

We all smiled.

I still believe in asking difficult questions. But I no longer assume that the person who says least has thought the least.

Frank doesn't need me anymore. He's doing for others what I once did for him, and doing it differently. That's not replication. It's the kind of growth that doesn't produce hierarchy.

Ancient Worlds, Modern Communities

Society for Classical Studies mini-grant · SCS blog

First team in mainland China to receive the SCS's Ancient Worlds, Modern Communities mini-grant. Three in-person seminars, two in Beijing backed by the Stanford Center at Peking University, one in Ningbo. An online panel with Edith Hall from Durham. The second Beijing session, co-hosted with the Asian Liver Center, placed Roman public bathing in dialogue with contemporary hepatitis B discrimination in Chinese employment law. Some participants signed up to volunteer with ALC afterward. I wrote an opinion piece on the SCS website. Pasts Imperfect, a scholarly newsletter on global antiquity, picked it up.

The real metric was not attendance. It was the moment when a participant who came for Cicero left with an ALC volunteer form.

Cross-City Civic Education Initiative

Ningbo · Nanjing · Shanghai · 2023–present

Eight student organizers from Ningbo, Nanjing, and Shanghai. No school affiliation, no faculty sponsor, no budget. Weekly meetings, reading groups, public seminars reaching roughly 120 participants. Migrant labor, disability rights, hepatitis B discrimination, criminal justice. A 2-day reading seminar for 30 aspiring student activists, featuring NGO leaders, grassroots organizers, and a college freshman building prisoner reentry through entrepreneurship. Eight cities, no hierarchy.

The organizational model was the thesis: horizontal, voluntary, deliberately unformalized. Every successful organization faces the pressure to calcify. Whether keeping it loose was wisdom or just youth, I'm still deciding.

Liberal Arts Review

Chair & editor-in-chief · Beijing 101 High School

I took a student journal and turned it into an institution. Launched the Insight Commons Fellowship with Princeton and Johns Hopkins tutors, 15 students working toward their first independent papers through Braudel, Scott, Shanghai on Strike. The most consequential move: merging 6 humanities clubs across 101's international and national departments. Two club leaders were skeptical. The negotiation, shared governance, resource-pooling, cross-department events, taught me more about institutional design than any paper I've written.

A journal is an institution. Treating it as one, with governance, partnerships, distribution, was my first experiment in building something that outlasts the founder.
