Requirements at a glance, phase one

← Back to portfolio

A summary of product requirements draft v0.1, written for working-group discussion. It is a draft and the site is not yet live. Statements about users are hypotheses to be tested, not research findings. The internal document is not linked.

The one-sentence plan

Phase one turns the official website into a trust-verification tool, so that a school leader or a local education office can understand Building Bridges in five minutes and feel safe contacting us. The student entry comes in phase two, after we interview past students.

The problem

The current site is a bilingual showcase page. Its recent content mostly links out to WeChat posts, and its share preview is empty. It says who we are. It does not answer what a decision-maker asks first when an invitation arrives from a program they have never worked with: is it safe, what is taught, what will it ask of the school, how do we cooperate. If the site cannot answer, the conversation usually stops there.

The decision is rarely one person's. The reader has to feel safe, and then be able to explain the decision to someone above them. The site has to support both.

What it deliberately does not do

  • Student accounts, course selection, and course evaluations.
  • A custom content management system. An existing site builder or CMS, so non-technical volunteers can update pages.
  • Online payments or memberships.
  • Any partnership or endorsement that has not been confirmed by the other side.

Rules for content, set before development

  • Every page has one content owner and one reviewer. A page is published only after review.
  • Students and alumni who appear in photos or video need explicit permission. No identifiable information about minors.
  • An alumni story needs the person's written consent, and a guardian's consent for a minor. The purpose and the audience are stated, and consent can be withdrawn.
  • A partnership appears only after the other side confirms it, in wording they have approved. Every number has a source.

Growth, as loops rather than traffic

BB does not need more visitors. It needs three loops to reinforce themselves: trust (real reviews from partner schools lead new schools and endorsers to reply), recruitment (volunteer stories and course materials bring new volunteers), and alumni return (a student becomes a teaching assistant, then comes back to teach, and becomes a visible example for the next cohort). The site exists to make those three turn.

A test of my thinking: how Notion grew

I used Notion's early growth to test these ideas, not as a template to copy. Interviews with people on Notion's team describe a pattern worth borrowing in spirit.

  • A loop, not a funnel. Notion users made templates, shared them, and brought in new users who made templates of their own. For BB the equivalent is past courses and alumni stories being seen, which brings new volunteers and partner schools, which produces new courses and stories.
  • Start with a small core, and treat them as members. Notion's ambassador program began with about twenty vocal users, and its team found a small, highly engaged community better than a large one in the early days. For BB, alumni ambassadors begin at ten to twenty people, and the interviews are the first step.
  • A few people who truly agree beat a lot of general attention. A program that involves minors should not chase traffic.
  • Do not overstate. This one is our own rule, not Notion's: only confirmed partnerships are shown.

Sources: Camille Ricketts on Notion's community-led growth (Dock) and Community-led growth at Notion (Decibel). Both are interviews with members of Notion's team. I have not checked them against Notion's own materials.

The biggest risk

Content and voice. If no one supplies material and no one is responsible for what is said publicly, the site is an empty shell. The draft ends with the decisions that have to be made first: who owns external statements, when and in what wording a partnership is shown, which safety policies can be public, which numbers can be public, who maintains the Chinese and English versions, and whether servers sit in mainland China or overseas.
